Lessons from the heston-momentum project
=======================================

I started this project hoping to beat the option market with a better equation plus some machine learning.
That mostly did not happen. But I learned a lot along the way, and these are the things I want to remember.


About markets
-------------

1. The market is hard to beat because everyone is looking at the same things.
   Momentum really does help predict how volatile a market will be next month. But option prices already
   include that. Once I added implied volatility to the model, momentum added nothing.

2. Returns are close to random. Volatility is not.
   I could not predict which way prices would go. But volatility clusters and calms down over time, and simple
   models catch that well. That is why option traders care so much about volatility.

3. "Making money" is not the same as "having an edge."
   Selling options and hedging them looked very profitable in my backtest. But most of that is a payment for
   taking crash risk. In 2008 it lost money. The profit is the reward for being willing to lose badly sometimes.

4. Check what price you are actually trading at.
   My hedging backtest priced options at the VIX. But VIX is above the price of at-the-money options, often by
   several points. On one day I checked, the gap was 4 points, more than my whole profit per trade. One wrong
   input can create a fake result.

5. Hedging is about smoothing, not about getting rich.
   Delta hedging cut the swings in my P&L by more than half. It did not add profit. It made the strategy
   survivable.


About models
------------

6. Simple structure beats extra features.
   Plain Heston mean reversion was the best way to forecast implied vol. Adding jumps (Bates) helped price the
   smile. Adding more and more variables mostly made things worse out of sample.

7. Machine learning needs the right setup, not just more power.
   Tree models trained on one market at a time lost to linear regression, because trees cannot predict past
   the highest value they have seen. When I trained one model on all markets, with everything scaled relative
   to implied vol, it caught up. The fix was the setup, not tuning.

8. Big AI models are not magic.
   Google's TimesFM, used straight out of the box, was worse than HAR, a simple regression from 2009. It only
   helped when I averaged it with HAR or corrected its bias. Fine-tuning did not help at all.

9. Small details can flip a result.
   TimesFM's default output is the median, not the mean. For volatility, which has big upward spikes, the
   median is too low, and my loss function punished that hard. Switching outputs changed the conclusion.


About doing research honestly
-----------------------------

10. Never let the test data leak into the model.
    Every model was refit once a year using only past data, with a gap of 21 days so no future returns slipped
    in. Without this, almost anything looks like it works.

11. Decide the rules before you look at the results.
    If you try ten ideas and report the best one, you will fool yourself. I wrote down every choice for the
    hedging test before running it. Ideas that came from looking at results get labeled "post-hoc."

12. A "no" result is only useful if the test could have said "yes."
    The Monte Carlo study showed some of my tests had only about 10% power. So "we found nothing" sometimes
    really meant "we could not have found it." That is worth knowing before claiming anything.

13. Use the right statistics.
    My targets overlapped day to day, so normal t-stats would have been far too confident. Newey-West
    corrections were needed everywhere.

14. One day of data is an anecdote.
    The smile result (32% better with jumps) came from a single day of option prices. It might hold, but I
    cannot say so until I have many days.

15. Sanity-check the machinery with fake data first.
    Before trusting the hedging backtest, I simulated a market where I knew the right answer. It came out
    right, so I could trust the code and focus on the data problem.


About the practical side
------------------------

16. Free data has limits.
    Yahoo has no history of option prices. Cboe vol indices had gaps, bad prints and stale stretches. Good data
    (OptionMetrics, tick data) costs money or needs a university license, and those licenses have rules: no
    sharing, no bulk downloading.

17. The cluster has its own rules.
    You cannot submit jobs from the gateway node. The venv needs its Python module loaded. A leftover
    PYTHONPATH killed my first 200-task job array. TimesFM needs an A100, not any GPU. Each of these cost me time.

18. Save everything you cannot get back.
    Option chain snapshots cannot be downloaded again later, so each one gets committed the day it is taken.

19. Write things down as you go.
    The README, the logs and the notes file meant I could stop and pick up again without redoing work.


If I had to sum it up in one line: the option market is smart, simple models do most of the work, and the
hardest part of research is not fooling yourself.
